T-cells find infected and cancerous cells by recognizing short peptides displayed on the cell surface by MHC molecules. If you could predict which peptide a given T-cell receptor recognizes, you could design better vaccines, pick better targets for cell therapies, and understand why some patients respond to immunotherapy and others do not. It is one of the more useful open problems in immunology.

The field has plenty of models for it. The trouble is that most of them are evaluated on epitopes they were trained on. Experimentally validated binding data covers only about two thousand epitopes, mostly from well studied pathogens, so a model can score well by memorizing the handful of targets it has already seen and still be useless on a new one. That is exactly the case that matters in the clinic, where the receptor is known and the target is not.

TCRBench is my attempt to make that distinction measurable. I aggregated T-cell receptor and epitope data from eight public databases, fifty published studies, and around 24,000 bulk repertoire samples, then standardized and deduplicated it into one dataset. On top of that I defined six prediction tasks covering the receptor alpha chain, beta chain, and paired chains, for both MHC class I and class II.

The part I care most about is the evaluation. Each task is scored under four conditions of increasing difficulty: unseen receptors against known epitopes, then entirely unseen epitopes, then unseen MHC alleles. The splits are built by clustering receptor and epitope sequences first and holding out whole clusters, so a test case cannot be a near copy of something in training. Those conditions are meant to mirror real situations: a new T-cell clone from a patient sample, a cancer neoantigen nobody has characterized, a patient carrying an MHC allele that is underrepresented in public data.

Running existing methods through it, the pattern is consistent. Classification based models do well on familiar epitopes and cannot score an unfamiliar one at all, since the epitope has to be one of their output labels. The transformer models can score anything in principle, but their performance drops sharply once the epitope is genuinely new. I also built a model, ESM2-QUEST, that starts from the ESM2 protein language model and is fine tuned on sequences from the benchmark. Starting from a protein language model instead of training from scratch on the small interaction set holds up much better when the epitope is unseen, which suggests the model is learning something about the sequences rather than the labels.

The dataset, the splits, and the code are open. The work sits in the [QUEST repository](https://github.com/shashidhar22/quest), with the benchmark on the [tcrbench2 branch](https://github.com/shashidhar22/quest/tree/tcrbench2). This was done with colleagues at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, and it grew out of QUEST, the T-cell specificity project I led in the Warren Lab.
